Executive Summary

5 Key Findings

1

Adopt (s,S) min-max as your default — not a plain reorder point

Your fixed per-batch COA/lab cost (€150–300) plus minimum freight is exactly the "general ordering cost" condition under which (s,S) is provably optimal. Simulation-tuned (s,S) beats textbook (r,Q) by 8–15% on total cost when both demand and lead time are stochastic. [4][5][11]

2

Size safety stock off the 90th-percentile lead time (~45–55 days), not the nominal 30 days

At 30-day-plus lead time, lead-time variance dominates demand variance in the safety-stock formula. Model your crypto-payment clearing (3–7 days) as an explicit stochastic component of σ_LT. Under-modeling lead time is the single largest stockout driver in your business. [12][14][19]

3

Run a high service level (92–97%) — your economics are asymmetric

With 60–80% gross margins, a stockout costs far more than an overstock. The newsvendor-optimal service level for a 5–10× cost-asymmetry sits around 83–90%; high-margin specialty guidance pushes A-items to 95–97% fill. Overstock risk is capped mainly by peptide shelf-life. [6][17]

4

Forecast with TSB (intermittent-demand), pooled across SKUs — never moving averages

With ~30 SKUs each seeing sparse, lumpy orders, per-SKU forecasting has too few events. Use TSB (Teunter-Syntetos-Babai, obsolescence-aware) as the practical workhorse. Expect 20–40% inventory reduction versus naive methods. [2][8][13]

5

Gate every replenishment through a Bucharest↔Ibiza transfer check before triggering a China PO

An inter-location transfer avoids a fresh €150–300 COA (Certificate of Analysis) cycle and 30-day pipeline. This pre-PO gate is the highest-ROI single rule you can implement this quarter. [23]

Section 1

SKU Classification: The ADI/CV² Decision Tree

Before choosing any reorder model, classify each of your ~30 SKUs by demand pattern using the standard ADI/CV² framework (Syntetos-Boylan classification), because the right forecasting method and reorder policy differ sharply by quadrant.

ADI

Average Demand Interval — average number of periods between non-zero demand events.

CV²

Squared coefficient of variation of nonzero demand sizes = (σ_demand / mean_demand)².

                 CV² < 0.49        CV² ≥ 0.49
ADI < 1.32   |  SMOOTH          |  ERRATIC
             |  (EOQ/ROP works) |  (ROP + fat safety stock)
ADI ≥ 1.32   |  INTERMITTENT    |  LUMPY
             |  (Croston/TSB)   |  (TSB + (s,S), Bayesian pooling)
For a peptide catalog, expect the majority of SKUs to land in INTERMITTENT and LUMPY — the two hardest quadrants, where MAPE/RMSE-tuned models actively mislead and you must weight forecast error by inventory cost impact (SPEC loss).

Action: Maintain an ADI/CV² column per SKU in your planning sheet, recomputed monthly.

Section 2

Core Reorder Models: When Each Fits

2a. (s,S) Policy — The Right Default for This Business

The (s,S) policy is formally proven globally optimal whenever ordering carries a fixed cost component including batch/lot costs (Wei & Yao 2020) — which is precisely your situation: every PO incurs a €150–300 COA test plus minimum freight regardless of order size.

s (reorder point)

When inventory position drops to s, place an order.

S (order-up-to)

Order enough to bring position back up to S.

(S − s) gap

Your effective batch size — widens as fixed inspection cost K_QA rises.

Verdict: DEFAULT for all intermittent/lumpy SKUs. Simulation-optimized (s,S) outperforms analytically-derived (r,Q) by 8–15% on total cost.

2b. Reorder Point (ROP) with Continuous Review

Best for smooth/erratic flagship SKUs. Continuous review beats periodic review when lead time far exceeds the review period.

ROP = (Average daily demand × Lead time) + Safety Stock
Verdict: Use for the few SMOOTH SKUs; otherwise upgrade to (s,S).

2c. Min-Max / Periodic Review

Min-Max is operationally just (s,S) with min = s and max = S — the most practical implementation form of the (s,S) recommendation.

Verdict: Implement (s,S) as a min-max table in a monthly-refreshed spreadsheet. Beats ERP for <50 SKUs.

2d. EOQ — Where It Helps and Where It Fails

EOQ helps answer "how big should the batch (S − s) be?" once you fold the COA test into the fixed order cost:

EOQ = √( 2 × D × (K_order + K_QA) / H )

D       = annual demand (units)
K_order = fixed cost per order (freight, admin)
K_QA    = COA/lab test cost per batch (€150–300)
H       = annual holding cost per unit
Verdict: Use to sanity-check batch size, then reconcile against MOQ (Minimum Order Quantity) and shelf-life. EOQ fails for lumpy SKUs and collapses when supplier MOQ exceeds EOQ.
Section 3

Safety Stock for Long Lead Times + Demand Variability

3a. The Master Formula

Use the combined demand-and-lead-time-variance safety-stock formula — at a 30-day lead time, the lead-time term dominates:

SS = Z × √[ (LT × σ_d²) + (D̄² × σ_LT²) ]

Z     = service-level Z-score
LT    = average lead time (days)
σ_d   = std dev of daily demand
D̄     = average daily demand
σ_LT  = std dev of lead time (days)   ← dominant term for 30-day LT
Service LevelZ ScoreUse For
90%1.28C-items, near-expiry SKUs
95%1.65B-items
97%1.88A-items / flagship movers

3b. Modeling the 30-Day Lead Time Realistically

Model your lead time as a sum of stochastic stages, not a single constant.

StageDurationNature
Crypto/international payment clearing3–7 daysstochastic
China supplier fulfillment5–10 daysstochastic + substitution risk
International freight14–21 daysstochastic (customs fat tail)
Receiving1–2 days~fixed
COA / lab testing5–10 daysstochastic (lab capacity)
Total~28–50 daysmean ≈ 33d, P90 ≈ 47–50d
 
σ_LT = √(Σ σ_stage²)   ← feed directly into master formula

Set reorder trigger on projected days-of-cover (on-hand + in-transit), not on-hand alone.

3c. COA Testing Cost as a De-Facto MOQ Floor

Your €150–300 per-batch COA (Certificate of Analysis) cost creates a minimum economic order quantity.

MOQ_QA_floor = K_QA / (target_QA_fraction × unit_gross_margin)

Example: K_QA = €200, unit margin = €40, cap QA at 10% of margin (€4/unit)
  → MOQ_QA_floor = 200 / 4 = 50 units minimum viable batch

Economic batch = max(EOQ, MOQ_QA_floor, supplier_MOQ), capped by shelf-life

For very slow SKUs where this implies years of cover, consider batch-pooling COA across multiple SKUs in one shipment or dropping the SKU.

Section 4

Intermittent Demand Forecasting for Peptide SKUs

4a. Croston → SBA → TSB Progression

Adopt the intermittent-demand progression, ending at TSB as your production method — the only one that handles obsolescence:

1
Croston — Decomposes demand into size and interval, forecasting each separately.
2
SBA (Syntetos-Boylan Approximation) — Corrects Croston's upward bias by × (1 − α/2). Always prefer SBA over raw Croston.
★
TSB (Teunter-Syntetos-Babai) — Updates demand probability every period so forecasts decay toward zero for dying SKUs. This is your default forecaster. Typical result: 20–40% inventory reduction.

4b. 2026 Frontier: GP-Tweedie and Hierarchical Bayesian TSB

Hierarchical Bayesian TSB

Pools parameters across SKUs — a slow peptide borrows statistical strength from similar ones. Bayesian update cycle aligns with monthly parameter refresh. [2]

GP-Tweedie Probabilistic

Handles zero-inflated sparse demand; outputs a full predictive distribution for direct quantile-based safety stock (no normality assumption). [1]

Train on SPEC loss, not MAPE/RMSE — the Stock-Oriented Prediction Error Cost weights errors by inventory-cost impact, aligning the model with profit. [9]

4c. Demand Segregation: True Customer Demand vs Internal Flows

Tag every inventory outflow with a reason code before fitting any model:

SALE          → feeds the forecast
TRANSFER      → excluded from forecast, tracked separately
SAMPLE        → excluded
GIFT          → excluded
SHRINKAGE/LOSS → tracked separately (adds to gross requirements)
TEST_CONSUMED → tracked separately (adds to gross requirements)

This is the highest-leverage data-hygiene step: a single mis-tagged internal transfer can distort a slow SKU's forecast for months.

Section 5

Multi-Location Replenishment: Bucharest Hub + Ibiza Spoke

Run a hub-and-spoke model: Bucharest receives all China POs (does COA), Ibiza is replenished by internal transfer. Always check for an inter-location transfer before triggering a supplier PO — a transfer avoids a fresh €150–300 COA cycle and the 30-day pipeline.

The Pre-PO Gate

When a location hits its reorder point s:
  1. Is there surplus (above its own s) at the OTHER location?
     → YES: transfer, if transfer_cost < COA_saved + margin_of_avoided_stockout
     → NO:  trigger a China PO to the hub

Key Advantage

Intra-EU transfers need no new COA (same tested lot), so minimum transfer quantity is far smaller than a China MOQ.

Safety-Stock Placement

Pool safety stock at Bucharest hub (risk-pooling reduces total SS via √ law). Ibiza lean with short-cycle transfers, sized off transfer lead time (~2–5 days), not 30-day China pipeline.

Section 6

Common Pitfalls

1. Using a single ROP for lumpy SKUs. Use DDMRP (Demand Driven Material Requirements Planning) buffer zones instead: Red = safety stock, Yellow = replenishment zone, Green = order-spike buffer.
2. Trusting the nominal 30-day lead time. Real effective LT is 30–55 days; sizing to the mean guarantees ~50% stockout probability. Size to P90.
3. Trusting ERP safety-stock modules blindly. For <50 SKUs, ERP is "garbage in, garbage out" without constant refresh — a monthly-refreshed spreadsheet beats it.
4. Not amortizing COA over batch size — ordering small multiplies your per-unit QA cost and destroys margin.
5. Ignoring shelf-life / lot expiry — cap every batch so it sells before expiry; use FEFO (First-Expiry-First-Out) picking; don't let (s,S) math order more than shelf-life allows.
6. Treating COA lab capacity as infinite — stagger reorder triggers to avoid hitting the lab with 10 batches at once.
7. Not modeling supplier substitution / short-ship risk — build a fulfillment-reliability factor into σ_LT.
8. Counting internal transfers/samples as demand — inflates forecasts on exactly your slowest SKUs.
