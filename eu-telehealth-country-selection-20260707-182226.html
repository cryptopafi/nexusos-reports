1. Purpose and Scope

This report compares EU and EEA (European Economic Area — countries using EU's single market but not full members, like Norway and Iceland) countries as potential locations to incorporate and operate a telehealth platform that includes prescription medicine dispensing and home delivery. It covers platform licensing, electronic prescription infrastructure, prescription mail-order legality, compounding pharmacy access, advertising rules, company formation ease, and health data rules.

Out of scope: Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) and research peptides. TRT involves controlled substances requiring additional scheduling layers. Research peptides lack EU marketing authorisation and cannot be lawfully prescribed via a compliant platform. These are correctly excluded from initial platform design.
Legal Disclaimer: Nothing in this report constitutes legal advice. It is illustrative context for business planning only. All conclusions must be confirmed with qualified local healthcare law counsel before any business decisions are made.

2. How EU Pharmacy Rules Work: Plain-English Overview

The 8-Country Mail-Order Zone

Only ~8 EU countries legally allow patients to order prescription medicines online and have them delivered to their home. These are: Germany, Netherlands, Estonia, Sweden, Denmark, Lithuania, Finland — plus Norway (EEA, not EU). Portugal is ambiguous (local delivery only domestically). All other 19+ EU member states explicitly prohibit it.

The Destination-Country Rule

When a pharmacy in Netherlands ships prescription medicine to a patient in another country, it is the destination country's law that controls legality — not the pharmacy's home country. A Dutch pharmacy can ship to Germany (allowed) but not to France or Ireland (prohibited). This is the most misunderstood rule in EU pharmacy.

Prescribing vs Dispensing — Two Separate Rules

Under Directive 2011/24/EU, a doctor licensed in any EU member state can prescribe to patients in any other EU country. That prescription is legally valid EU-wide. But dispensing and shipping still follow the destination country's rules. The prescribing rule and the dispensing rule are independent.

The EU-Wide Advertising Ban

Article 88 of EU Directive 2001/83/EC bans direct-to-consumer (DTC) advertising of prescription medicines across all EU countries. You cannot run ads promoting a specific prescription drug. Disease awareness campaigns (describing a condition without naming a drug) are allowed. Germany is the strictest enforcer by far.

4. Top-3 Ranked Recommendations

1
🇳🇱 Netherlands — Start Here
Best overall: mail-order RX established, DocMorris origin country, world-class compounding hub, English-language regulators

The single most decisive fact: DocMorris — the company whose 2003 ECJ (European Court of Justice) case defined the legal framework for cross-border prescription medicine sales across the entire EU — operated from the Netherlands. Redcare Pharmacy, currently the largest publicly-listed online pharmacy in Europe, originated here. Fagron, the world's largest pharmaceutical compounding company, is headquartered in Rotterdam. The Netherlands is not merely a permissive environment for prescription mail-order; it is the operational birthplace of European prescription e-commerce.

The absence of a dedicated telehealth law is an advantage at this stage. A platform connecting patients with doctors and routing prescriptions to a licensed pharmacy qualifies as an "information society service" under EU Directive 2000/31/EC (the e-Commerce Directive) — a lighter regulatory classification than being classed as a healthcare provider. Doctors consulting via the platform need BIG-register (Beroepen in de Individuele Gezondheidszorg — Dutch professional healthcare register) registration, but they need not be physically based in the Netherlands. EU-licensed doctors may provide cross-border telemedicine legally under EU professional recognition rules.

The addressable mail-order market from a Netherlands pharmacy base includes Germany (largest EU pharmacy market, €50B+ total retail), Sweden, Denmark, Finland, Estonia, and Lithuania — a combined population of roughly 170-180 million EU residents. The Netherlands-based pharmacy ships prescription medicines directly to their homes. For patients in France, Spain, Ireland, and other mail-order-prohibited countries, the platform can still provide doctor consultations and prescriptions — patients collect medicine locally. This model is commercially realistic for conditions like erectile dysfunction (ED) where home delivery privacy matters most to the core addressable market (Germany, Netherlands, Nordics).

The IGJ (Inspectie Gezondheidszorg en Jeugd — Health and Youth Care Inspectorate) and CBG (College ter Beoordeling van Geneesmiddelen — Medicines Evaluation Board) both publish English-language documentation, reducing compliance friction for non-Dutch founders. BV (Besloten Vennootschap — private limited company) formation requires no minimum capital, can be done quickly, and operates in an English-strong Amsterdam startup environment. Note: Fellos, a Dutch men's health telehealth startup focused on ED, raised investment in 2025 — cited as illustrative context; specific financial details are beyond knowledge cutoff and UNVERIFIED.

2
🇩🇪 Germany — Best Market, Not Best Base
Best expansion market for Netherlands-based operator; complex for foreign founders to incorporate in directly

Germany is the largest prescription medicine retail market in the EU — total pharmacy retail exceeds €50 billion annually, with the mail-order segment growing sharply after the eRezept (electronic prescription) system reached nationwide rollout in 2024. eRezept makes Germany's prescription flow more automated and digital than any other EU country except Estonia. For a Netherlands-based operator, Germany is the essential first expansion market: a Netherlands pharmacy can ship prescription medicines directly to German patients, and the eRezept integration (via the TI — Telematikinfrastruktur, Germany's national health data network) can be handled by a German pharmacy partner who manages TI connectivity while the platform routes prescriptions via a commercial API.

The trade-offs of incorporating primarily in Germany are significant for a foreign non-German founder. GmbH (Gesellschaft mit beschränkter Haftung — limited liability company) formation requires €25,000 minimum capital. Registration takes weeks to months. Regulatory filings — with BfArM (Bundesinstitut für Arzneimittel und Medizinprodukte — Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices) and with Landesapothekerkammern (state pharmacy boards) — are primarily in German. The HWG (Heilmittelwerbegesetz — therapeutic advertising law) is the strictest healthcare advertising regime in the EU, with §9 and §10 heavily enforced and litigated even for telehealth consultation marketing, not just drug advertising.

For a private-pay telehealth platform, the March 2025 statutory insurance (GKV — gesetzliche Krankenversicherung) rule requiring telemedicine doctors to be based in Germany does not apply. Private-pay telehealth remains accessible from a Netherlands operational base, with German-patient demand served by cross-border prescription mail-order. Germany is best treated as the primary market destination, not the primary incorporation country.

3
🇪🇪 Estonia — Best Tech Base, Not Standalone Pharmacy
World-class digital infrastructure; too small market and too gated for standalone pharmacy operation

Estonia has the most advanced digital health infrastructure in Europe per capita. 99.9% of all prescriptions have been issued electronically via the national X-Road (a shared data exchange infrastructure across Estonian government services) since 2010. The e-Residency program allows foreign founders to incorporate an OÜ (Osaühing — limited liability company) remotely in 1-3 days, with English-language government services, and the corporate tax structure imposes 0% on retained earnings (20% only when profits are distributed). Ravimiamet (State Agency of Medicines) is the regulator and has English web presence.

The decisive constraints are market size and access gates. Estonia's population is 1.3 million people — smaller than many European cities. Only 5 pharmacies held e-pharmacy distance-selling licences as of 2024. More critically, accessing Estonia's X-Road e-prescription infrastructure as a private foreign telehealth platform requires formal registration as a healthcare provider with Ravimiamet — it is not a free digital pass. Cross-border e-prescription exchange currently operates between Estonia and Finland (operational since 2019) and is gradually expanding via the EU xt-EHR project, but it does not yet constitute a general EU-wide e-prescription gateway.

Estonia's strongest strategic role is as a tech and compliance base within a dual-incorporation structure: an Estonia OÜ handling platform technology, data processing, and EU compliance engineering, while a Netherlands BV (Besloten Vennootschap — private limited company) manages the pharmacy partner relationship and cross-border prescription mail-order logistics. The EHDS (European Health Data Space) regulation mandating e-prescription interoperability across all EU member states by 2029 will eventually reduce Estonia's relative digital advantage as the whole EU converges toward a connected system — but until then, Estonia is an excellent digital operations base.

5. Countries Removed From Contention

Hard Blockers (Mail-Order RX Prohibited)

  • Ireland: Mail-order RX explicitly illegal per HPRA (Health Products Regulatory Authority). Hard structural blocker. English-language and easy company formation are irrelevant when the core pharmacy model is illegal.
  • Belgium: Prescription mail-order to patients' homes prohibited. Adjacent to Netherlands but cannot receive cross-border prescription shipments.
  • Romania: Prescription mail-order prohibited. Low cost base does not compensate for pharmacy restriction and less-developed compounding ecosystem.
  • Poland: Prescription mail-order prohibited, with enforcement and policy tightening further in 2026.

Insufficient Evidence / Practical Constraints

  • Cyprus: Mail-order RX status UNVERIFIED. Assumed prohibited by pattern. Pharmacy infrastructure too thin. Must verify with local counsel and Pharmaceutical Services of the Ministry of Health.
  • Malta: Same situation as Cyprus. UNVERIFIED. Very small domestic market. Pharmacy infrastructure insufficient. Must verify with MPIRA (Medicines and Poisons Authority).
  • Portugal: AMBIGUOUS. Domestic home delivery restricted to local municipality. Cross-border distance selling under DL 171/2013 covers OTC only. Cannot be relied on for planning until clarified with INFARMED.
  • Sweden: Mail-order RX allowed, but strict advertising culture (IMY, MPA enforcement) and high cost base make it suboptimal as an incorporation country for ED/weight-loss telehealth. Better as a receiving market served from Netherlands.

6. What "Higher-Risk / Likely Excluded" Means for TRT and Peptides

TRT (testosterone replacement therapy) involves testosterone — a controlled substance subject to scheduling or anabolic/narcotic restrictions in most EU member states. In addition to standard pharmacy licensing, a platform including TRT would need controlled-drug licensing layers that vary significantly across EU countries and are not covered in this analysis. Including TRT in an initial platform launch substantially increases regulatory complexity — correctly excluded from initial scope.

Peptides that are not licensed medicines — the majority of what are called "research peptides" — exist in a regulatory grey zone across the EU. They cannot lawfully be prescribed or dispensed as medicines via a compliant telehealth platform because they have not completed the EMA (European Medicines Agency) approval process or national equivalent. A compliant platform may only prescribe and dispense medicines with a valid marketing authorisation in the relevant country. These exclusions are the correct initial design decisions.

7. Practical Next Steps for the Netherlands Path

Legal & Regulatory
  1. Engage Dutch healthcare law firm. Key questions: BV formation + IGJ notification requirements; BIG-register for partner doctors; platform-as-information-society-service vs healthcare provider classification.
  2. Partner with a registered Dutch mail-order pharmacy holding IGJ authorisation for prescription distance selling. This pharmacy is your dispensing partner — do not attempt to operate a pharmacy directly as a first step.
  3. Confirm doctor network model — Netherlands BIG-register or EU-licensed cross-border doctors? Confirm contractual structure between platform, doctors, and pharmacy with counsel.
  4. Clarify platform classification — "information society service" under Directive 2000/31/EC (e-Commerce Directive) or "healthcare provider" under Directive 2011/24/EU? Classification determines which licensing rules apply and which country's regulator has jurisdiction.
Operations & Market Expansion
  1. Identify Netherlands-based compounding pharmacy if GLP-1 receptor agonist (e.g. semaglutide in compounded form) is in product roadmap. Magistral formula (individual patient compounding) remains legal under EU Pharma Package rules; confirm specific indication rules with counsel.
  2. Plan Germany as first expansion market. Identify German pharmacy partner with Versandhandelserlaubnis (BfArM mail-order permit). Dutch-licensed doctors prescribe; German pharmacy partner handles eRezept and TI connectivity.
  3. Build disease-awareness marketing. Article 88 of EU Directive 2001/83/EC bans prescription drug DTC advertising across all EU. Market the consultation service and condition awareness — not specific drugs. Retain Dutch advertising law firm to review all creative before launch.
  4. Implement GDPR + WGBO compliance from day one. Patient health data is special-category under GDPR Article 9. Appoint a DPO (Data Protection Officer) if core activity involves large-scale processing of health data.
  5. Monitor EHDS 2029 deadline. EU Health Data Space mandates e-prescription interoperability across all EU member states by 2029 — this will materially expand addressable mail-order markets. Build prescription architecture with this horizon in mind.
  6. Do NOT ship prescription medicines to France, Spain, Belgium, Ireland, Poland, Romania, or other mail-order-prohibited countries. Patients in those markets receive doctor consultations and a valid prescription; they collect medicine locally.
